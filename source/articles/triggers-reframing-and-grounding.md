# Triggers, reframing and grounding

**Covers:** Trigger, Reframing, Grounding

## Three tools for hard moments {#overview}
Some situations set off strong feelings almost every time, and you may have already noticed which ones they are for you. Spotting them gives you a head start. Once the feeling begins, two techniques help. One changes how you think about the situation. The other settles the body when anxiety is high. In this article we connect all three: know what sets you off, change the story, and calm the body.

## Trigger {#trigger}
A trigger is a specific thing that reliably sets off a strong emotional reaction in you, in a pattern that repeats. It might be a certain tone of voice, a type of message, being interrupted or being ignored. The reaction can seem out of proportion to what actually happened. That is because the trigger is touching something older, such as a past experience or a deep need.

Finding your triggers is mostly a matter of watching for patterns. The next time you react strongly, write down what happened right before, what you felt in your body and what you did. After a few entries, the same situations will appear again. Then you can prepare. You can plan a pause, choose another moment for a hard conversation, or ask for what you need.

## Reframing {#reframing}
Reframing means deliberately looking for a more accurate and less catastrophic way to see a stressful situation. The facts stay the same. What changes is the meaning you give them. If you think "I completely failed that presentation", your body prepares for danger. If you think "one slide went wrong and the rest was fine", the stress falls and you can plan the next step.

A reframe is not forced positivity, and it has to be true. Three questions help you test it: "What would I tell a friend in this situation?", "What is the evidence for this thought, and against it?" and "What else could explain this?" The method comes from cognitive behavioral therapy, where it is called cognitive restructuring.

## Grounding {#grounding}
When anxiety is high, thinking your way out is hard. Grounding works through the senses instead. You notice, on purpose, what your senses are picking up right now. That pulls your attention away from spiraling thoughts about the future and anchors it in the present, where you are usually safe.

A well-known version is the 5-4-3-2-1 exercise: name five things you can see, four things you can touch, three things you can hear, two you can smell and one you can taste. Other versions use slow breathing, pressing your feet into the floor or holding a cold glass. Grounding works best when you practice on calm days, so that it feels familiar when you need it.

## A worked example {#example}
You get an email from your manager: "Can we talk tomorrow?" with no further detail. Let's go through it together.

1. **Trigger.** You notice the reaction: a tight stomach and the thought "I am in trouble". You recognize it, because short, vague messages from authority always do this to you.
2. **Grounding.** Your heart is racing, so you stop and do 5-4-3-2-1. You see a lamp, a cup, a window, a book and a plant. Your breathing slows.
3. **Reframing.** You ask: what is the evidence? "She often asks for quick chats about scheduling. Last week it was about holidays." A more accurate thought is "I do not know what it is about, and most of the time it is routine."
4. **Action.** You reply: "Sure. Is 10 a.m. good? Happy to prepare something if it helps."

The whole sequence takes about two minutes. The email did not change, but your response to it did.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is using reframing to deny a real problem. If something went wrong, a good reframe says so and looks for a useful next step, not "everything is fine".

A second slip is trying grounding for the first time in the middle of a panic. Practice while you are calm.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A trigger is a repeatable situation that sets off a strong reaction. Track yours to see the pattern.
- Reframing finds a more accurate way to see a situation, and it has to be true.
- Grounding uses the senses to bring your attention back to the present.
- Notice the trigger, calm the body, then check your thoughts.
