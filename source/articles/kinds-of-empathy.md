# Kinds of empathy, and protecting it

**Covers:** Cognitive empathy, Emotional empathy, Perspective-taking, Empathy fatigue

## More than one way to understand someone {#overview}
When a friend tells you something painful, what do you actually do? You may work out why they feel that way. You may feel a lump in your own throat. You may try to picture the situation from where they stand. These are different things, and we call all of them empathy. There is a fourth point too: caring for others uses energy, so you need a way to protect it. Knowing which part is working helps you choose a better response.

## Cognitive empathy {#cognitive-empathy}
Cognitive empathy means understanding what another person feels and why, without necessarily feeling the same emotion yourself. It is a mental read of their situation: "She is nervous because the presentation is tomorrow and her manager will be there."

This is very useful at work. Negotiators, teachers and managers use it to predict how someone will react and to choose a good way to speak. Used alone, though, it can come across as cold. A person who understands your fear perfectly but shows no warmth does not feel helpful. Skilled manipulators also have good cognitive empathy, which is why the other kinds matter.

## Emotional empathy {#emotional-empathy}
Emotional empathy is the part you feel in your body. When a friend cries and your own throat tightens, that is emotional empathy: you feel, to some degree, what they feel. It builds closeness and tells the other person that you care.

It has a cost, though. If you absorb every feeling in the room, you can become overwhelmed and less able to help. A person who panics because a patient is panicking cannot calm the patient. Aim to feel enough to connect, and keep enough distance to act.

## Perspective-taking {#perspective-taking}
Perspective-taking is trying to see the situation through the other person's real point of view, with their experience, worries and limits. This is different from imagining what you would feel in their place. You are not them, and your reactions come from your own history.

The practical way is to ask instead of assuming: "What is this like for you?" Then listen. You can also write down what the person knows, wants and fears. Often a disagreement shrinks when you see that the other person is working from different information or under different pressures.

## Empathy fatigue {#empathy-fatigue}
If you absorb other people's emotions all day, you eventually run low. That exhaustion is empathy fatigue. It is common among nurses, teachers, support staff and anyone who listens to distress for a living. Signs include feeling numb, irritable, avoiding people and caring less than you used to.

It does not mean you are a bad person. It means your emotional resources are drained, and protecting them is what makes empathy sustainable. Some steps that help: set limits on how much you take on, take real breaks, talk to someone about your own load, move from feeling with someone to caring for them, and do something restful afterward.

## A worked example {#example}
A coworker, Tom, tells you his project was cancelled after six months of work. Let's compare four responses and see what each one uses.

| Response | Kind of empathy | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| "That makes sense. You put a lot into it, so it must feel like a loss." | Cognitive | Shows understanding, but can feel detached |
| (Your voice drops and you sigh with him) | Emotional | Shows you feel it too, which builds closeness |
| "What was this project like for you day to day?" | Perspective-taking | Opens the door to his view |
| (Later, you take a walk after a long day of listening) | Protects against fatigue | Keeps you able to help tomorrow |

The best help usually mixes the first three, so that Tom feels understood, accompanied and listened to. The fourth step is not selfish. If you do it every day, you can keep supporting people for years.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to think that more empathy is always better, and that you should feel everything others feel. That road leads to exhaustion.

A second slip is stopping at understanding. Knowing how someone feels is not the same as showing it. A short, warm sentence often matters more than a perfect analysis.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Cognitive empathy understands a feeling, and emotional empathy shares it. Good support uses both.
- Perspective-taking means seeing the situation from their side, which is best done by asking.
- Empathy fatigue comes from absorbing too much for too long.
- Setting limits and resting are part of caring for others, not a failure of it.
