# Listening and reading nonverbal cues

**Covers:** Active listening, Nonverbal cue

## What people say and what they show {#overview}
Think about the last time someone told you "I'm fine" in a voice that said the opposite. You believed the voice, not the words. Words carry only part of what a person means. The rest comes through how they speak, how they sit and what their face is doing. To understand people accurately, you need to listen to the words and to watch the signals around them. In this article we will do both, and we will also see how far you can trust what you see.

## Active listening {#active-listening}
Most of us listen while quietly preparing our reply. Active listening is the opposite: you listen to understand, and you do not wait for your turn. Here is a test. Could you explain the other person's point of view in a way that they would accept as accurate?

Some habits that build the skill:
- Give your full attention: put the phone away and face the speaker.
- Let them finish without interrupting, and tolerate a pause.
- Say back what you heard: "So you are worried that the deadline is unrealistic?"
- Ask open questions such as "How did that feel?"
- Notice the emotion as well as the facts.

Saying it back is powerful for two reasons. It gives the speaker a chance to correct you, and it shows that you care enough to check.

## Nonverbal cue {#nonverbal-cue}
A nonverbal cue is a signal that carries emotional meaning without words: tone of voice, speed of speech, posture, gestures, eye contact and facial expression. Often it tells you more than the words do. Someone who says "I am fine" with a flat voice and averted eyes is sending two messages that do not match.

When words and cues disagree, treat that as a prompt to ask a gentle question. It is not proof of what the person feels.

## Reading cues carefully {#careful}
Cues are clues, not facts. Crossed arms can mean defensiveness, but they can also mean the person is cold or comfortable. A lack of eye contact can mean shyness, respect in some cultures or distraction. Three habits help you stay honest:

1. **Find the baseline.** Notice how the person normally behaves. A change tells you more than a single signal.
2. **Look for clusters.** One cue means little. Several pointing the same way mean more.
3. **Check, do not assume.** Say "You seem quiet today. Is everything all right?" and listen.

Cultures differ as well. Eye contact, personal space and loudness carry different meanings in different places.

## A worked example {#example}
A teammate, Jun, says in a meeting: "Sure, I can take the extra task." You notice his voice is quiet, his shoulders are down and he looks at the table. Let's go through it step by step.

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| Notice the cues | Quiet voice, slumped shoulders, no eye contact: they do not match "Sure" |
| Compare with the baseline | Usually Jun speaks up and jokes, so this is a change |
| Look for a cluster | Three signals point toward reluctance or stress |
| Ask after the meeting | "You said yes quickly. Does the extra task fit with your workload?" |
| Listen actively | He says two other deadlines are due this week. You say back: "So you are stretched and did not want to say no in front of everyone?" |

Without the cues, you would have heard a yes and moved on. Without the checking step, you might have guessed wrongly. Together they led to a conversation and a fairer plan.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to believe in fixed meanings, such as "crossed arms always mean closed". You may also have heard that most communication is nonverbal. That statistic is often misused. It came from small studies of feelings when words and tone disagreed, and it does not describe everyday speech.

A second slip is half-listening while you plan what to say next. The speaker usually notices.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Active listening aims to understand, and saying back what you heard lets the speaker correct you.
- Nonverbal cues, such as tone, posture and expression, often carry the emotion.
- Treat cues as clues. Use the baseline, look for clusters and check by asking.
- Culture changes what many cues mean.
