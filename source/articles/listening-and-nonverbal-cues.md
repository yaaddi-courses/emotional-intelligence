# Listening and reading nonverbal cues

**Covers:** Active listening, Nonverbal cue

## What people say and what they show {#overview}
Words carry only part of what a person means. The rest comes through how they say it, how they sit and what their face is doing. To understand people accurately you need to listen to the words and to watch the signals around them. This article covers both, and a warning about how far you can trust what you see.

## Active listening {#active-listening}
Active listening is listening with the goal of understanding the other person, not waiting for your turn or planning your reply while they speak. The test is simple: could you explain their point of view in a way that they would accept as accurate?

Practical habits:
- Give your full attention: put the phone away and face the speaker.
- Let them finish without interrupting, and tolerate a pause.
- Reflect back what you heard: "So you are worried that the deadline is unrealistic?"
- Ask open questions such as "How did that feel?"
- Notice the emotion as well as the facts.

Reflecting is powerful because it gives the speaker a chance to correct you, and it shows that you care enough to check.

## Nonverbal cue {#nonverbal-cue}
A nonverbal cue is a signal that carries emotional meaning without words: tone of voice, speed of speech, posture, gestures, eye contact and facial expression. Often it reveals more than the words do. Someone who says "I am fine" with a flat voice and averted eyes is giving you two messages that do not match.

Pay special attention when the words and the cues disagree. That is a prompt to ask a gentle question, not proof of what the person feels.

## Reading cues carefully {#careful}
Cues are clues, not facts. Crossed arms can mean defensiveness, but also cold or comfort. A lack of eye contact can mean shyness, respect in some cultures or distraction. Three habits keep you honest:

1. **Find the baseline.** Notice how the person normally behaves. A change is more informative than a single signal.
2. **Look for clusters.** One cue means little. Several pointing the same way mean more.
3. **Check, do not assume.** Say "You seem quiet today. Is everything all right?" and listen.

Cultures differ as well. Eye contact, personal space and loudness carry different meanings in different places.

## A worked example {#example}
A teammate, Jun, says in a meeting: "Sure, I can take the extra task." You notice his voice is quiet, his shoulders are down and he looks at the table.

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| Notice the cues | Quiet voice, slumped shoulders, no eye contact: they do not match "Sure" |
| Compare with the baseline | Usually Jun speaks up and jokes, so this is a change |
| Look for a cluster | Three signals point toward reluctance or stress |
| Ask after the meeting | "You said yes quickly. Does the extra task fit with your workload?" |
| Listen actively | He says two other deadlines are due this week. You reflect: "So you are stretched and did not want to say no in front of everyone?" |

Without the cues, you would have heard a yes and moved on. Without the checking step, you could have guessed wrongly. Together they led to a conversation and a fairer plan.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Believing in fixed meanings, such as "crossed arms always mean closed". A famous statistic that says most communication is nonverbal is also misused. It came from small studies of feelings when words and tone disagreed, and it does not apply to everyday speech. A second mistake is half-listening while you plan what to say next.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Active listening aims to understand, and reflecting back lets the speaker correct you.
- Nonverbal cues, such as tone, posture and expression, often carry the emotion.
- Treat cues as clues. Use the baseline, look for clusters and check by asking.
- Culture changes what many cues mean.
